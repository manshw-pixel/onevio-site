A QBR template for customer success teams

A good QBR takes 45 minutes and has six parts: a goals recap, usage and outcomes, a support summary, the roadmap, next-quarter goals and actions. It is a review of the customer's results, not a sales pitch. Use the agenda below, bring the right data, and follow up within 48 hours.

The QBR agenda

Send this agenda to the customer a week before the meeting, so they can add to it. Keep to the times. The last two parts matter most, so protect them.

PartMinutesPresenter
1. Goals recap5CSM
2. Usage and outcomes10CSM, with the customer
3. Support summary5CSM
4. Roadmap10CSM or product lead
5. Next-quarter goals10Customer, with the CSM
6. Actions5CSM
  • Goals recap. Read back the goals you agreed last time. Say plainly which were met and which were not.
  • Usage and outcomes. Show how the product was used and what it changed for the customer. Ask them to fill in the business result if you cannot see it.
  • Support summary. Tickets raised, what was fixed and what is still open. Own the bad news before they raise it.
  • Roadmap. Only the items that matter to this customer. Do not promise dates you cannot keep.
  • Next-quarter goals. Two or three goals the customer cares about, each with a way to measure it.
  • Actions. Who does what by when, on both sides. Read them back before the call ends.

What to bring

Prepare from data, not memory. Four things cover most QBRs:

  • Health score: the account's current score and what moved it since the last review. See the health score guide if you do not have one yet.
  • Usage: active users and key actions compared with what was bought, including unused licenses.
  • Support tickets: count, themes and anything still open.
  • Revenue: for your own prep, know the account's value and whether it grew or shrank. The NRR calculator shows retention across your base. This number is for you, not for the slides.

Cadence by account size

Not every account needs a review every quarter. A simple rule:

  • Large or strategic accounts: quarterly.
  • Mid-sized accounts: every six months.
  • Smaller accounts: once a year, timed a few months before the renewal.

Whatever cadence you pick, set the next date at the end of each QBR. An account with no next date is the one that gets missed.

Running it so it isn't a pitch

Customers stop coming to QBRs that feel like sales meetings. Keep the focus on their results:

  • Let the customer talk for at least half the time. Ask questions in parts 2 and 5.
  • Leave pricing and upsell out of the agenda. If growth comes up, book a separate call.
  • Keep the deck short. Ten slides is plenty, and fewer is better.
  • Invite the budget owner. A QBR with only day-to-day users cannot agree goals.

Follow up within 48 hours

Send a short written summary within two days. Include the goals you agreed, each action with an owner and a date, and the date of the next QBR. Log the meeting against the account. Then check the actions at your next regular call, not at the next QBR.

Mistakes that waste a QBR

  • A product demo instead of a review. The customer came to talk about their results.
  • Hiding problems. If they know about a bad month, say it first.
  • Goals nobody measures. "Improve adoption" is not a goal. "60 active users by March" is.
  • No follow-up. Actions without owners and dates do not happen.
  • No next date. The gap between QBRs grows until one is skipped before a renewal.

FAQ

QBR questions

What is a QBR in customer success?

A QBR, or quarterly business review, is a meeting between you and a customer's decision makers to review the results they got from your product, agree goals for the next period and settle open issues. Despite the name, it does not have to happen every quarter.

How long should a QBR be?

Forty-five minutes is enough for most accounts. That covers a goals recap, usage and outcomes, a support summary, roadmap, next-quarter goals and actions. If you need more time, the meeting is probably trying to do too much.

How often should you hold QBRs?

Match the cadence to the account. Large or strategic accounts often meet quarterly, mid-sized accounts every six months and smaller accounts once a year, usually ahead of the renewal. Some small accounts do not need a formal QBR at all.

Who should attend a QBR?

On the customer side, the person who owns the budget and the person who runs the product day to day. On your side, the CSM, plus the account manager if commercial topics will come up. Keep it small enough for a real conversation.

What should happen after a QBR?

Send a short written summary within 48 hours: the goals you agreed, the actions, who owns each one and by when. Log the QBR against the account and set the date of the next one before you close the meeting notes.
